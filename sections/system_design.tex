\section{System Design \& Task Setup}
\label{sec:system_design}

\subsection{Hardware}

Experiments were run on a da Vinci Research Kit (dVRK)~\cite{dvrk}. The
dVRK has a da Vinci S surgeon console connected to da Vinci Si
patient-side arms, fitted with bipolar fenestrated forceps. Two
patient-side arms, PSM1 and PSM2, can be controlled; trials can use
either arm alone or both together.

\subsection{Formative Study}
\label{subsec:formative_study}

Before this work, the author helped collect data for a separate,
ongoing study led by Mary Kate Gale (one of the mentors of this
thesis), on "fine wrist control as a marker of surgical teleoperation
expertise." That study collected data from 27 novices (no surgical or
medical experience) and 9 experts (robotic surgeons with more than 250
hours of laparoscopic teleoperated experience), using an earlier
version of the task board used here.

That data collection led to several findings that shaped this thesis.
The camera views participants preferred informed the vision setup
(Section~\ref{subsec:vision}). Participants also said they were
frustrated using only one arm, PSM1, with no clutch and no way to move
the camera. Separately, novice participants moved less smoothly, which
repeatedly broke pegs on the board.

High success rates, together with this peg breakage, led to a new task
board with shorter pegs, meant to make the task harder while breaking
fewer pegs. That new board has been built but is not used in this
thesis: the experiments in Section~\ref{subsec:task} use the original
board. Instead, the new board and these findings support the
closed-loop, difficulty-adaptive task proposed as future work
(Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Task}
\label{subsec:task}

The task is to move cylinders between pegs. Each cylinder is two-toned,
blue on the bottom face and white on the top, so the same cylinder can
be placed blue-side-down or white-side-down depending on the trial.
Depending on the trial, one or all four cylinders are in play at once.
At rest, cylinders sit on four center yellow pegs, 3D-printed in PLA.

Eight more pegs sit around the outside of the board, 3D-printed in
clear resin (V4) so each can be lit from inside. Each outer peg has an
embedded LED that lights up blue or white to show the target cylinder
and where it should go next.

Each trial follows a fixed sequence (Figure~\ref{fig:task_sequence}).
At the start, two lights of one color turn on, one on each side of a
cylinder. Cylinders always rest blue-side down; the light color tells
the participant which side should face down once the cylinder is
placed. When the participant lifts the correct cylinder, one light
turns off and the other stays on, marking the target peg. The
participant moves the cylinder to that peg and places it, which turns
the remaining light off. For blue-light trials, the cylinder is moved
directly, with no rotation; for white-light trials, the cylinder must
be inverted first.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/Task.png}
\caption{One trial, step by step. (A) Both lights (blue) turn on on one row. 
(B) The participant lifts the cylinder; one light turns off,
one stays on to mark the target. (C) The cylinder is placed on the lit
peg, which then turns off, completing the trial. (D) After a 2-second
pause, the next trial starts, this time with both lights on the
another row (white).}
\label{fig:task_sequence}
\end{figure}

The board is electrified with conductive paint, giving real-time
detection of cylinder placement and removal: touching a cylinder to a
peg closes a circuit for that peg. This gives a direct, ground-truth
signal for whether a cylinder is on a peg or in transit, without
relying on the vision pipeline.

\subsection{Vision}
\label{subsec:vision}

The main camera is the dVRK endoscope, mounted on the ECM. It gives
the stereo view the surgeon sees at the console and feeds the pipeline
live.

An Intel RealSense D405 depth camera was mounted next to the ECM
(Figure~\ref{fig:ecm_pedals}). Its depth stream is not used live;
instead, it is used post-hoc for the SAM2-based 3D localization
described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}. Alongside the D405, a second
camera, a Logitech HD1080 webcam, was tried as an optional live second
view for the participant, at either the top or the side of the task
pad, with an extra pedal to switch to it during the task. This live
second-view setup was tested but not adopted into the final system.

\subsection{Sensing}
\label{subsec:sensing}

Three more kinds of data support the vision data above. Kinematic
data, joint positions, velocities, and tool-tip position, is read
directly from the patient-side arms (PSMs). An Arduino-based circuit
detects when a task starts and ends, separate from the vision and
kinematic data. Contact force is measured by an ATI force sensor under
the task pad, which picks up force as cylinders are placed and removed
(Figure~\ref{fig:ecm_pedals}, panels D and E).
