\section{Background \& Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Post-Hoc Analysis}

The most common approach derives skill proxies from recordings taken
during the trial. The JIGSAWS dataset~\cite{jigsaws} is the standard
benchmark: kinematic and video recordings of surgeons performing
bench-top tasks, paired with expert skill ratings. Studies that use the
kinematic side of this data compute features such as path length,
velocity, and jerk from the recorded trajectories, then correlate them
with the expert ratings to separate novice from expert performance.
Studies that use the video side instead apply object detection to track
task progress directly from what the camera sees, catching information
kinematics alone cannot, such as object identity, object state, and
spatial relationships between objects and the task environment; the
computational cost is significant, since detection and segmentation pipelines 
that are accurate enough to track a task's progress are typically too heavy to 
run at camera frame rate.

Either way, these proxies are computed after the trial ends. The
pipeline reads a complete trajectory or video, not a live stream. None
of this feedback reaches the trainee while the trial is still running.

\subsection{Real-Time Multimodal Skill Prediction}

The closest related work is Hu et al.~\cite{hu2026}, who combine
kinematic and vision data from the da Vinci system to predict surgical
skill in real time. They predict one general OSATS skill score for a
suturing task, not task-specific metrics.

Their results show that combining kinematic and vision data works
better than using either alone, that predictions track the surgeon's
movements over time, and that models trained on expert demonstrations
generalize better than models trained on novice ones. But two
gaps remain. First, they predict one general skill score, not the
task-specific measures this thesis targets (Section~\ref{subsec:scope}).
Second, their model can predict from live data in principle, but they
only test it on recorded trials, not on a robot running in real time.

\subsection{Real-Time Feedback Attempts}

Laca et al.~\cite{laca2022} ran a randomized trial of real-time feedback
on a robotic tissue dissection task, on a physical da Vinci Xi.
Trainees who got feedback in the second of two rounds lowered their
error rate and raised their skill score more than a no-feedback group,
with the biggest gains among low-performing trainees. But the performance 
measure behind the feedback comes from four human raters
reviewing recorded video after the trial. So the feedback is real-time,
but the measurement behind it is not. \\
