\section{Background \& Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

Related work on surgical task assessment splits into four streams. Each
stream solves part of the problem this thesis addresses. None solves all
of it live, on the physical robot, without a human rater.

\subsection{Post-Hoc Kinematic Analysis}

The dominant paradigm derives skill proxies from recorded robot
kinematics. The JIGSAWS dataset~\cite{jigsaws} is the standard benchmark:
kinematic and video recordings of surgeons performing bench-top tasks,
paired with expert skill ratings. Studies built on this paradigm compute
features such as path length, velocity, and jerk from the recorded
trajectories, then correlate them with the expert ratings to separate
novice from expert performance.

These proxies are informative, but they are computed after the trial
ends. The pipeline reads a complete trajectory, not a live stream. None
of this feedback reaches the trainee while the trial is still running.

\subsection{Post-Hoc Vision}

A second stream applies object detection to recorded video, to track task
progress directly from what the camera sees rather than from
robot-reported kinematics. This catches information kinematics alone
cannot -- object identity, object state, and spatial relationships
between objects and the task environment.

The cost is compute. Detection and segmentation pipelines accurate enough
for task-progress tracking are typically too heavy to run at camera frame
rate. Reported systems in this stream run offline or with latency too
high for live feedback.

\subsection{Real-Time Multimodal Skill Prediction}

The closest technical precedent to this thesis is Hu et
al.~\cite{hu2026}, who fuse kinematic and vision data from the da Vinci
Surgical System to predict surgical skill level in real time. Their
target is Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS)
score on a suturing task, not task-specific metrics.

They report three analyses. First, on model design: fusion models that
combine kinematic and vision features consistently outperform either
modality alone, measured by mean Spearman correlation. Second, on
real-time behavior: predictions tracked over time correlate with the
surgeon's gestures. Third, on skill-stratified training: models trained
on expert-level demonstrations generalize better than models trained on
novice-level ones, and generalize best to participants of similar skill.

This work confirms the technical premise this thesis builds on --
fusing kinematic and vision data gives a more stable signal than either
modality alone. Two gaps remain. The predicted target is a general OSATS
skill score, the kind of global judgment this thesis deliberately avoids
(Section~\ref{subsec:scope}). And "real time" here means the model can
predict causally from data up to the current time step; the evaluation
itself runs on recorded trials, not a system deployed live during an
ongoing one. This thesis deploys the equivalent pipeline live, on the
physical robot, during the trial itself.

\subsection{Real-Time Feedback Attempts}

The strongest evidence for this stream comes from Laca et
al.~\cite{laca2022}, who ran a randomized controlled trial of real-time
feedback on a robotic tissue dissection task, on a physical da Vinci Xi.
Trainees who received feedback in the second of two rounds lowered their
error rate and raised their skill score significantly more than a
no-feedback control group, with the largest gains among initially
low-performing trainees.

This result is closest in spirit to this thesis: it is real-time, and it
runs on physical hardware, not simulation. But the trial's own
performance measure -- error tally and skill score -- comes from
retrospective review of recorded video by four blinded human raters. The
feedback loop is real-time; the measurement behind it is not. This thesis
automates that measurement step too: performance measures are computed
live, from sensor and vision data, with no human rater at any point in
the loop.
